Creatine and Alzheimer's disease: New review finds mostly animal studies and only one small study in patients

A new scoping review found nine studies on creatine in Alzheimer's disease. Eight were in animals, and the only human study was a small pilot study with no control group. The authors say the evidence is preliminary.

Interest in creatine and brain health has grown in recent years. A new review by researchers at the University of Cádiz in Spain argues that, for Alzheimer's disease, that interest has run ahead of the science.

What the researchers did

The review was published in the journal Nutrients on 23 October 2026. It is a scoping review. It maps what research exists and where the gaps are, and it does not pool results to measure an effect. The researchers searched five databases for animal and human studies in which creatine was given in Alzheimer's disease.

They found nine studies. Eight of them were in mice or rats.

The animal studies: some promising findings, but mixed

Several animal studies found less oxidative stress, less inflammation and better results on memory tests. The findings are still mixed:

  • In two studies of male rats injected with amyloid-beta, a protein that builds up in Alzheimer's disease, creatine led either to no change or to worse results on memory tests.
  • In one study of genetically modified mice, females and males responded differently.
  • Many studies used models in which disease-like damage is caused quickly, for example by a single injection. According to the authors, these models are a poor match for the slow course of the disease in humans.

The authors also point to reporting errors. One animal study states that the mice weighed "20 kg". This is probably a typo, but it leaves it unclear which animal was actually used.

The human study: 20 patients, no control group

The only human evidence was one pilot study from the University of Kansas Medical Center (NCT05383833). Its results have been published in five papers by the same research group. According to the trial registry, 20 older people with Alzheimer's disease took creatine for eight weeks. Everyone received creatine, and no group received a placebo.

The research group reported that creatine levels in the blood and brain rose, and that participants did better on several cognitive tests after eight weeks. Handgrip strength increased, while leg strength did not change. Some brain measures of nerve cells and antioxidant defences did not change either.

The review authors write that the study showed the approach was feasible and that patients appeared to tolerate the supplement. It cannot, however, show that creatine caused the changes. Without a control group, the effect of creatine cannot be separated from things like practice effects from repeated testing or participants' expectations. A study of 20 people over eight weeks also says nothing about long-term effects or rare side effects.

Six gaps in the research

The authors list six weaknesses in the research so far:

  1. Many animal studies use models that are a poor match for the human disease.
  2. Differences between the sexes are poorly studied.
  3. There are no studies of other ways of giving creatine that might reach the brain better.
  4. There are no independent randomised controlled trials. All human research comes from one research group.
  5. No one has studied whether creatine can prevent the disease.
  6. Frailty and physical function in patients are barely studied.

They conclude that research on creatine in Alzheimer's disease is preliminary and that randomised controlled trials are needed.

Limitations of the review

The review has weaknesses of its own, and the authors name several. The search was narrow: it covered only Alzheimer's disease, not dementia in general, and left out studies where Alzheimer's disease was only a secondary outcome. Relevant studies may have been missed. A scoping review also does not assess how large any effect is.

The work was funded by the European Regional Development Fund (FEDER) and the regional government of Andalusia, and the authors declare no conflicts of interest.

What this does not show

The review does not show that creatine helps against Alzheimer's disease, or that it prevents it. It shows that the question has hardly been studied in humans. A search of ClinicalTrials.gov on 20 November 2026 for creatine and Alzheimer's disease returned only the Kansas pilot study.
