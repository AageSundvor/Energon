New meta-analysis: Creatine changes a common kidney test – the trials showed no signs of kidney damage in participants

A new meta-analysis of 26 randomised trials finds that creatine supplements raise blood creatinine, but the trials showed no signs of kidney damage in participants during the periods studied – and important groups have not been studied.

Creatine is one of the most studied dietary supplements, yet questions about what it does to the kidneys have followed it for years. A new systematic review and meta-analysis, published on 27 July 2026 in the journal International Urology and Nephrology, pools the randomised trials that have measured kidney-related markers. Creatine changed a common kidney test, but the trials showed no signs of kidney damage in the participants – healthy people and some patient groups – during the periods studied. That does not mean the question is settled for everyone.

What is creatine?

Creatine is a nitrogen-containing compound that the body makes from amino acids. We also get it from food, mainly animal products. When creatine breaks down in the body, it forms creatinine, the same substance that is often measured in blood to estimate how well the kidneys filter.

What did the researchers find?

The researchers, from institutions including the Federal University of São Paulo in Brazil and the University of Kansas in the US, included 26 randomised, placebo-controlled trials with a total of 1,036 participants. The literature search covered studies up to March 2025.

The main findings were:

  • Blood creatinine rose: On average, it was 0.14 mg/dL higher than with placebo.
  • Estimated kidney filtration (GFR) fell when calculated from creatinine, by an average of 10.75 mL/min.
  • Measured kidney filtration showed no clear change: In the trials that measured GFR with the tracer Cr-EDTA, which is not affected by creatine, there was no statistically significant difference.
  • Other markers showed no clear differences overall. These were blood urea, albumin and protein in the urine, and urinary creatinine.

The researchers interpret the changes in creatinine-based tests as likely reflecting altered creatinine metabolism rather than kidney injury. Taking creatine produces more creatinine, so a standard blood test can look worse even if the kidneys are not necessarily working worse. The authors write that blood creatinine may be an unreliable marker of kidney health in people taking creatine.

The analysis also included two small trials in patients on haemodialysis. In those trials, creatinine rose while urea fell.

Limitations and uncertainty

The findings apply only to the groups and periods studied, and the authors point out several limitations themselves:

  • Large variation: Results for the main outcome varied considerably between trials, partly because of one outlier study and differences in body mass index.
  • Study quality: Eleven of the 26 trials were judged to be at low risk of bias. Fourteen raised "some concerns", and one was at high risk.
  • Little data on a better marker: Cystatin C, considered a more suitable marker of kidney function, was measured in only two trials.
  • No data on chronic kidney disease without dialysis: There are no trials in people with chronic kidney disease who are not on dialysis (stages 1–5). The authors say this group carries the greatest theoretical risk and that their conclusions cannot be extended to it.
  • Few, small dialysis trials: The two dialysis trials were too small to rule out risk.
  • Short follow-up: Only four trials lasted longer than six months.
  • Kidneys only: The meta-analysis looks only at kidney markers, not at other possible side effects. Creatine is also often used alongside other substances, which can make kidney findings outside trials hard to interpret.

The authors declare no competing interests. The publication fee was paid by the Brazilian research agency CAPES.

The researchers call for larger, better studies, especially in people with advanced kidney disease.

EU rules on health claims

The EU has its own rules on which health claims may be used for creatine and other food supplements, set out in, among others, Regulation (EU) No 432/2012. The new meta-analysis does not change those rules.
