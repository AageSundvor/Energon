Creatine during immobilisation and rehabilitation: New review finds mixed and uncertain results

A new systematic review of six small studies in young, healthy people did not find that creatine prevented loss of muscle size while an arm or leg was immobilised, and the authors say the research does not support firm conclusions.

When an arm or a leg has to be kept still for a few weeks, for example in a cast after an injury, the muscles shrink. Creatine has been proposed as a possible strategy during such periods. A new systematic review in the journal Experimental Physiology looked at what the research actually shows, and the answer is that the evidence is mixed and thin.

What the researchers did

Researchers from the UK, Canada and the US searched the PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science and Cochrane Library databases up to March 2026. They found six randomised, placebo-controlled trials that met their criteria. Together the trials had 118 participants, with mean ages between 15 and 24 years. In every trial, an arm or a leg was immobilised for one to two weeks.

Because the trials were few and very different from each other, the authors did not pool the numbers in a meta-analysis. Instead they described the findings trial by trial.

What the review found

  • Muscle size: Two trials measured the cross-sectional area of the thigh muscle with CT or MRI. Both found roughly the same loss with creatine as with placebo, for example −5.6 percent versus −5.5 percent in one trial. The authors call this the most consistent finding of the review: creatine did not prevent the loss of muscle size in the leg.
  • Lean mass: Two other trials found a smaller loss of lean mass in the creatine group. They used DXA or bioelectrical impedance. The authors point out that these methods can be affected by how much water the body holds, and creatine can increase the water content of muscle. The difference may therefore reflect water rather than more muscle tissue being preserved.
  • Muscle function: The results were inconsistent and differed between arm and leg studies.
  • Rehabilitation: Four trials also followed participants after the immobilisation period. Two of them reported greater progress in the creatine group on some measures. The authors write that the small number of trials and the differences between them mean no firm conclusions can be drawn.

The authors conclude that current evidence does not unanimously support creatine as an effective countermeasure against muscle loss when a body part is immobilised.

Limitations

  • Few, small trials. Six trials with 118 participants in total is little. The authors note that several of the trials with favourable findings were among the smallest, and that two of them had some concerns about risk of bias.
  • Only young, healthy participants. None of the trials studied older or ill people. These are exactly the groups that often lose the most muscle during bed rest and immobilisation and take the longest to recover. How creatine works in them has not been studied in this body of evidence.
  • Short periods and old studies. Immobilisation lasted only one to two weeks, and several of the trials are more than 15 years old.
  • Different methods. The trials used different ways of immobilising the limb, different dosing protocols, different rehabilitation and different measurement methods. That makes them hard to compare.
  • Side effects are not covered. The review deals with muscle outcomes and says nothing about side effects or how participants tolerated the supplement.
  • Conflicts of interest. Three of the five authors report ties to the creatine industry, including advisory roles with manufacturers and receiving free creatine for studies. The review had no external funding.

What the review does not show

The review does not show that creatine helps or harms after injuries, surgery or bed rest. Nor does it say anything about older adults, patient groups or longer periods of inactivity. The authors call for new trials in exactly these groups, and for studies that also measure how much creatine the muscles actually take up.
