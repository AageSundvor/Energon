Creatine and the brain: two new small studies in women, and a review that urges caution

Two small, exploratory studies in women found some differences in cognitive tests and sleep measures with creatine, while a new review argues that research on creatine and brain function does not yet give firm answers.

Interest in creatine has long centred on muscle, but in recent years researchers have increasingly looked at the brain. Over the past few weeks, two new small studies in women and a critical review of the field have been published. The studies found some differences between creatine and placebo, but their authors call the findings exploratory. The review concludes that it has not yet been shown that creatine provides a measurable benefit for brain function.

Why study creatine and the brain?

The brain uses a lot of energy, and creatine is part of a system that helps cells keep their energy supply stable. Rare inherited disorders in which the body cannot make or transport creatine cause severe neurological problems, which shows that creatine matters for the brain. The open question is whether extra creatine in people without such a deficiency changes anything that can be measured in everyday life.

Study 1: younger and older women

A study published on 2 November 2026 in Biological Research for Nursing followed 48 physically active women in Brazil, 26 younger and 22 older. They were randomly assigned to creatine or placebo for 28 days and took a battery of cognitive tests before and after.

According to the abstract, creatine was associated with better results on several of the tests in both age groups, most consistently for reaction time, inhibitory control and working memory, and most clearly in the older women.

The authors themselves describe the test battery as exploratory and write that the findings need to be confirmed in new trials that test a pre-specified hypothesis. The groups also differed in how much creatine they got from their diet at the start, which the researchers adjusted for in the analysis.

Study 2: women in perimenopause

A US study from the University of North Carolina, published on 24 October 2026 in the European Journal of Applied Physiology, examined 51 women in perimenopause (average age 46). The trial is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov. Participants were assigned to creatine, placebo or a control group that did not train. The creatine and placebo groups did high-intensity resistance training twice a week for six weeks.

The findings were mixed:

  • Cognitive tests: Reaction time on a common attention test (Stroop) improved over time, but there was no difference between the groups.
  • Prefrontal oxygenation: Measured with light through the scalp, oxygenation during the test rose with creatine and fell with placebo.
  • Sleep: Sleep consistency, measured with a WHOOP wristband, was higher in the creatine group than in the placebo group after six weeks.
  • Symptoms: The creatine group reported fewer days of temperature sensitivity than the placebo group.

Here, too, the authors call the analysis exploratory. The groups were small, with 18 participants in the creatine group and 20 in the placebo group. The study declares conflicts of interest: one researcher is a scientific adviser to companies that make or sell creatine products, holds shares in one of them and has received research funding from industry. Another is an employee of WHOOP, which makes the wristband, and carried out the sleep and symptom analyses. According to the paper, he did so without knowing who received what, and the analysis code is public. The analyses have still not been repeated by an independent researcher.

The review: "plausible, but not proven"

In a review published on 23 October 2026 in Experimental Physiology, Marco Machado of Fundação Universitária de Itaperuna in Brazil examines why a strong biological rationale has not yet produced convincing clinical results. Machado is also a co-author of the Brazilian study described above. He declares no conflicts of interest.

His main points are:

  • The brain takes up little: In the few studies that have measured brain creatine with MR spectroscopy, levels generally rose by around 3 to 10 per cent, and some studies found no change.
  • The measurements are uncertain: The usual measuring method has historically had an uncertainty of the same size as the changes researchers are trying to detect. Newer, better methods are still rarely used.
  • Many tests, small groups: When many cognitive tests are run at once without a pre-specified main outcome, some differences can appear by chance. Many studies have only a few dozen participants.
  • Conflicting reviews: Some meta-analyses have found benefits for memory, but more recent critical appraisals point to weaknesses in several of them.

Machado argues that further research is still worthwhile, particularly in sleep deprivation, ageing and some diseases. He calls for pre-registered trials with a clear main outcome, more participants and direct measurement of creatine in the brain. His conclusion is that creatine should for now be regarded as an interesting hypothesis, not as a proven aid for brain function.

What the research does not show

  • None of the new studies shows that creatine prevents or slows dementia or age-related cognitive decline.
  • The studies lasted four and six weeks. They say nothing about long-term use.
  • The abstracts we have read do not report side effects. The studies were too small and too short to say anything reliable about rare or long-term side effects.
  • The findings apply to physically active women in specific age groups and cannot simply be extended to others.

EU rules on health claims

The EU has rules on which nutrition and health claims may be made about food and food supplements, set out in, among others, Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006. The studies described here do not change those rules.
